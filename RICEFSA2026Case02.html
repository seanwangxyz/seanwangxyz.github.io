STOP AND THINK

Chipotle's margin is double Starbucks', yet Starbucks turns its operating assets faster. What about selling coffee instead of burritos would produce exactly that pattern? Think about who makes your order, and how much of Starbucks' revenue comes from licensed stores that someone else owns.

Who finances these companies?

Every dollar of assets is paid for by someone. Read each bar left to right: customers, suppliers, employees, landlords, lenders, shareholders. Starbucks' shareholders sit below zero: buybacks and dividends have returned more than the company ever retained, so everyone else is funding the business plus the hole.

STOP AND THINK

If you have money sitting on a Starbucks card or in the app right now, you have lent Starbucks money at zero percent and cannot get it back in cash. Customers hold $1.75 billion of that float. Is that an operating liability or a financing one, and what would change in RNOA if you called it financing?

Q7 · Q8 · Sort both balance sheets

Sort it yourself

Each line is either operating (it runs the restaurants) or financial (it raises or parks money). The sort drives everything: NOA, NNO, NOPAT and RNOA recompute as you click. It opens on the case key's sort. Clear it and try it cold, then compare.

// APPLIED AI

The model that found a 102.5% return

An AI sorting Chipotle reported RNOA of 102.5%, and the number traveled into slides, a quiz bank and a rubric before anyone caught it. Reproduce it: on Chipotle, set both operating lease liabilities to Operating. Then look at the balance-sheet check under the readout.

It still ties. NOA − NNO = common equity holds under any sort in which every line lands somewhere, including a wrong one. The identity proves your arithmetic, not your classification. What catches the error is judgment: a lease liability is a promise to pay a landlord fixed amounts over 14 years, which is debt in everything but name, and a restaurant chain earning 100% on its operating assets should make you suspicious before it makes you excited.

The habit to keep: never ask a model to check its own answer in the same window. Open a fresh one and ask the question cold. Self-review inherits the first answer; a cold re-ask does not.

Revenue grew 5.4% only because of new restaurants. Fewer people walked into the stores Chipotle already had. A 27% RNOA built on shrinking traffic is a return you should test, not extrapolate.

STOP AND THINK

A new CEO's first-year charge is the cheapest big number they will ever take: it lands on the predecessor's watch in the market's memory and makes every later year look better. Argue it both ways. When is an $892M charge honesty, and when is it a cookie jar?

Beyond the case · the payoff

Would the deal make sense?

Chipotle earns 26.9% on its net operating assets and Starbucks earns 16.2%, so buying Chipotle should lift Starbucks' return. Set a price and find out why it does the opposite.

Analyst estimates in the press: William Blair about $300M; the FT about $770M.
Return on the price paid
Combined RNOA
Combined average NOA

The purple point is the combined company. It keeps a respectable margin but its turnover collapses, because the purchase price lands in NOA.
Why it happens

RNOA is computed on book value. An acquirer pays market value. The difference between the price and Chipotle's book NOA becomes goodwill and stepped-up assets, which are operating assets, and they sit in NOA permanently. You do not inherit a 26.9% business. You inherit a 26.9% business at 26.9%-business prices, and the return on that price is what Chipotle's own shareholders were already earning. That is why Chipotle's holders were happy on 8 October: the price transfers the return to the sellers.

Assumptions: Chipotle's year-end NNO of $3,794.2M is assumed by the buyer; NOPAT adds with no financing cost for the deal; savings taxed at 22%, the case's rate; Starbucks' average NOA of $15,373.3M. Illustrative, not a valuation.

STOP AND THINK

A falling RNOA is not by itself proof that the deal destroys value. The real test is whether the return on the price paid beats what Starbucks' investors could earn elsewhere at similar risk, which is the cost of capital. Hold that thought for the valuation half of the course. For now: at what price does the return on purchase price reach 8%, and is anyone going to sell Chipotle for that?
